## How it works

**Simple:** Every time a trade loses, the next trade is twice as big, so one win pays back all the losses. On FX it often means adding more and more to a losing position.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like doubling your bet at roulette after each loss: it works until you hit a long losing streak or the table limit, and then you lose everything at once.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

A position-sizing scheme, not a signal. Classic martingale doubles size after each loss; FX 'averaging' adds a new lot every X pips against the position and closes the whole basket at a small profit from the average price. It turns many small wins into a rare, very large loss, so the backtest equity curve looks smooth until the account is wiped out. Expected value is unchanged by sizing if the entry signal has no edge.

</details>

### The rule

Common EA logic on EUR/USD: open 0.01 lot in the direction of a simple signal; if price moves 20 pips against it, open another position of double size in the same direction; close all positions when the basket shows a net profit of 10 pips on the average price. Repeat. On daily bars, apply the same logic with levels set in ATR units.

### Data needed

- Daily (or intraday) FX prices
- Swap rates, since baskets are often held for weeks
- Margin rules (for example a 30:1 cap) to model forced liquidation

### Why it might work

None. Proponents say currencies 'always come back'; there is no evidence they must, and adding size after losses only changes the shape of the return distribution, not its mean.

### Why it might fail

A trend that runs further than the grid's capacity, like the October 1998 yen move that Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen (2008) describe, forces a margin call and a loss larger than all earlier wins combined. We found no academic study of FX martingale returns; ESMA's figures on retail CFD losses cover such traders but do not isolate them.
