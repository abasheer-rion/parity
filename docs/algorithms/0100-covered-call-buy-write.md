## How it works

**Simple:** Own the S&P 500 and every month sell someone the right to buy it from you at today's price. You keep the fee, but you give up any gain above that price.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like renting out a spare room: steady rent, but if a buyer offers a fortune for the house that month, you have already promised it at a fixed price.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

Cboe's BXM index holds the S&P 500 and writes the near-term at-the-money call, generally on the third Friday of each month, holding it to expiry and cash-settling. Index options are usually priced above the volatility that follows (the volatility risk premium), so the written calls earn more on average than they pay out.

</details>

### The rule

On each monthly option expiry, hold the S&P 500 and sell one at-the-money one-month S&P 500 call per unit held; keep the premium; repeat at the next expiry.

### Data needed

- Cboe BXM index daily history (free CSV)
- S&P 500 total return for comparison

### Why it might work

Investors pay up for index options as insurance, so option sellers earn a premium for bearing crash risk (the volatility risk premium).

### Why it might fail

The upside is capped, so it lags in strong bull markets and still takes most of a crash. The index ignores costs and taxes. Retail funds and bots often present the premium as 'yield', which it is not: it is payment for giving up gains.
