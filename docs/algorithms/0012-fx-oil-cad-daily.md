## How it works

**Simple:** Canada sells a lot of oil. On days when oil goes up, the Canadian dollar usually goes up too. The trick is whether yesterday's oil move tells you anything about today's currency move.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like a shop whose takings rise on days the weather is good; knowing today's weather helps, knowing yesterday's helps much less.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

Regress daily changes in USD/CAD on daily oil price changes over a rolling window. Same-day oil changes give a strongly significant out-of-sample fit at daily frequency (but not monthly or quarterly). With lagged oil changes the predictive ability is ephemeral and shows up only in some subsamples (around 2006-2007) once instability is allowed for.

</details>

### The rule

Each day, estimate a rolling regression of daily CAD/USD returns on the prior day's oil return; if the fitted forecast for tomorrow is positive go long CAD vs USD, if negative go short, holding one day.

### Data needed

- Daily USD/CAD spot
- Daily WTI or Brent oil price (e.g. FRED DCOILWTICO)
- Optional: Canadian and US short-term interest rates

### Why it might work

Oil is Canada's main commodity export, so oil price shocks move Canada's terms of trade and the Canadian dollar (Ferraro, Rogoff and Rossi 2015).

### Why it might fail

The authors find the lagged relationship is ephemeral and the link vanishes at monthly and quarterly frequencies, so a tradable edge is unlikely after costs. Kohlscheen et al. (2017) also find lagged commodity prices beat the random walk in only a few countries.

### After publication

Kohlscheen, Avalos and Schrimpf (2017, IJCB) confirm the same-day link for CAD (RMSE ratio 0.850 vs random walk, 2009-2015) but find lagged commodity prices add little forecasting power, consistent with the original paper.
