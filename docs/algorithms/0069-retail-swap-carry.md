## How it works

**Simple:** Borrow in a currency with low interest, like the yen, and hold one with high interest, like the Australian dollar. Your broker pays you the difference every night.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like borrowing money from a friend who charges almost nothing and putting it in a bank that pays a lot, as long as the exchange rate does not move against you.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

The retail version is usually one pair (AUD/JPY, NZD/JPY, USD/TRY) held long for the positive overnight swap, often with high margin. It is a concentrated form of the G10 carry factor. Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen (2008) show carry returns are negatively skewed because positions unwind suddenly when risk appetite and funding dry up.

</details>

### The rule

Each month, go long the pair with the largest positive interest differential among a small menu (for example AUD/JPY, NZD/JPY, USD/JPY), hold while the broker's swap is positive, and switch if another pair's differential becomes larger. No hedging.

### Data needed

- Daily FX spot (FRED H.10)
- Short-term interest rates per currency
- The retail broker's swap rates, which include a markup over the interest differential

### Why it might work

Uncovered interest parity fails on average, so high-yield currencies have tended not to depreciate enough to offset the rate gap; Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen (2008) explain the premium partly as compensation for crash risk.

### Why it might fail

A single pair has no diversification and the crash risk does not diversify away even across portfolios (BNP 2008). They cite the dollar's sharp fall against the yen on 7 and 8 October 1998 without fundamental news. Retail swaps add a broker markup, and 30:1 margin turns a carry crash into a margin call.
