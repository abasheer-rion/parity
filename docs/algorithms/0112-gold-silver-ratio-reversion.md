## How it works

**Simple:** Count how many ounces of silver buy one ounce of gold. When it takes far more than usual, swap gold for silver; when it takes far fewer, swap back.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like two siblings who usually walk together: when one runs far ahead, bet that the gap closes.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

If log gold and log silver prices are cointegrated, deviations of the ratio from its long-run level should shrink. Escribano and Granger test this on 1971 to 1990 monthly data with error correction models; cointegration holds only in some periods and needs shifts in the long-run level, and the forecasting gain does not survive out of sample for silver.

</details>

### The rule

Our simple version, for testing: each month, compute log(gold / silver) and its z-score against the past 5 years; above +2 hold silver and short gold, below -2 the reverse, close at the mean. This rule is ours.

### Data needed

- World Bank Pink Sheet monthly gold, silver and platinum prices (free, DAT-0035)

### Why it might work

Both are precious metals priced by many of the same investors, and some industrial and jewellery demand can switch between them, so their prices should not drift apart forever.

### Why it might fail

The source paper finds the relation unstable: cointegration needs breaks in the long-run level, and out of sample the silver forecasts lose to a random walk. The ratio's long-run level has shifted, so 'normal' may not exist. Silver is far more industrial than gold.
