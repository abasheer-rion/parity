## How it works

**Simple:** Mark the highest and lowest price during the quiet Asian night. When London opens and price breaks above that box, buy; if it breaks below, sell.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like waiting for the school bell: the hallway is calm until the bell rings, and then everyone rushes one way.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

An opening range breakout on session boundaries: define the range from roughly 00:00 to 07:00 London time on GBP or EUR pairs, place a buy stop a few pips above the high and a sell stop below the low, cancel the other order when one fills, target 1 to 2 times the range, stop at the opposite side. The claimed edge is the jump in volume and volatility when European dealers arrive. We found no peer-reviewed test of this exact rule.

</details>

### The rule

On GBP/USD or EUR/USD 5-minute bars: range = high and low from 00:00 to 07:00 London time. At 07:00 place a buy stop at high + 3 pips and a sell stop at low - 3 pips; the first fill cancels the other; stop at the opposite end of the range; take profit at 1 x range or close at 16:00 London.

### Data needed

- Intraday FX bars (5-minute or finer) with timestamps
- Spread at the London open, which widens at 07:00

### Why it might work

Volatility is lower in Asian hours and higher when London opens, so breakouts may follow through. Osler (2002, NY Fed staff report) shows stop-loss orders cluster at certain levels and that trends become unusually fast when those levels are hit, which is one plausible mechanism for breakouts running.

### Why it might fail

We found no academic test of the session breakout itself; claims come from forum backtests with unknown spreads and fills. Osler finds the stop-loss effect lasts hours, not days, and the open is exactly when retail spreads widen, so a small edge may not survive costs. False breakouts that reverse into the range are common.
