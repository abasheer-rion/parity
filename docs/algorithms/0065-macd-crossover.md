## How it works

**Simple:** Take a fast average and a slow average of price and look at the gap between them. When that gap starts growing upward past its own average, buy; when it turns down past it, sell.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like watching a car's speed rather than its position: you act when it starts to speed up or slow down.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

MACD is EMA(12) minus EMA(26) of price; the signal line is a 9-period EMA of MACD. The retail rule buys when MACD crosses above the signal line and sells when it crosses below; a variant (MACD(12,26,0)) trades the zero line. Chong and Ng (2008) and Chong, Ng and Liew (2014) test these on stock indices.

</details>

### The rule

Daily: MACD = EMA12(close) - EMA26(close); signal = EMA9(MACD). Go long when MACD crosses above the signal line, exit (or go short) when it crosses below. The academic variant MACD(12,26,0) uses the zero line instead of the signal line.

### Data needed

- Daily closing prices (SPY, FX spot)
- Cost estimate

### Why it might work

It is a smoothed momentum measure, so any edge would come from short-term trend persistence. Chong, Ng and Liew (2014) say their results 'shed some light on investors' belief' in it, not that a risk premium explains it.

### Why it might fail

Results in Chong, Ng and Liew (2014) are uneven across markets: the rule was significant in Milan and Toronto but not everywhere, which is what one would expect from chance across many market tests. The 12/26/9 parameters were never chosen with out-of-sample testing, and signal-line crossovers trade often, so costs matter.
