# Global imbalances currency strategy

## In plain words

Buy the currencies of countries that owe the rest of the world a lot, especially in foreign money, and sell the currencies of countries that are big lenders. Borrowers have to pay investors more to hold their currency.

## How it works

**Simple:** Buy the currencies of countries that owe the rest of the world a lot, especially in foreign money, and sell the currencies of countries that are big lenders. Borrowers have to pay investors more to hold their currency.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like a lender charging a higher rate to a customer who already owes a lot in a currency they do not earn.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

Sort currencies by net foreign assets to GDP, then within groups by the share of external liabilities in domestic currency, into five portfolios. Long the debtor portfolio with foreign-currency liabilities, short the creditor portfolio with domestic-currency liabilities. The signal updates once a year.

</details>

### The rule

Once a year, rank countries by net foreign assets / GDP (most negative = debtor) and by the share of their external liabilities in their own currency (low = more risk). Long the most indebted with foreign-currency liabilities, short the biggest creditors. Hold with monthly forwards.

### Data needed

- Net foreign assets and liability currency shares by country (External Wealth of Nations data, Lane and Milesi-Ferretti; Benetrix et al.)
- Monthly spot and forward rates

### Why it might work

Investors want compensation for holding the currency of a net debtor that borrows in foreign money, because that currency tends to fall in bad global times.

### Why it might fail

Balance sheet data arrive with a lag of a year or more, so using the year they describe instead of the year they were published adds look-ahead. Much of the return may overlap with carry, and we found no outside replication.
