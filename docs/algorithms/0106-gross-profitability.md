# Gross profitability (quality) premium in stocks

## In plain words

Buy companies that make a lot of gross profit for the size of their balance sheet and sell those that make little. Profitable firms have earned more, even though they look expensive.

## How it works

**Simple:** Buy companies that make a lot of gross profit for the size of their balance sheet and sell those that make little. Profitable firms have earned more, even though they look expensive.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like preferring the busy restaurant over the empty one even though its shares cost more.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

Gross profits to assets is revenue minus cost of goods sold, divided by total assets. Sorted on this, profitable firms earn higher average returns than unprofitable ones despite higher valuation ratios, and combining profitability with book-to-market improves value strategies, especially among large, liquid stocks.

</details>

### The rule

Each year after accounts are published, rank stocks by gross profits to assets; hold the top group long and the bottom group short, rebalanced monthly or yearly.

### Data needed

- Company accounts (revenue, cost of goods sold, total assets)
- Monthly stock returns
- The Kenneth French library's RMW factor is a free, related operating-profitability series

### Why it might work

Gross profit is a cleaner measure of a firm's earning power than net earnings, and the market seems to underprice it.

### Why it might fail

Quality factors became popular after 2013, so the premium may have shrunk. The free RMW factor uses operating, not gross, profitability, so it is not the same test.
