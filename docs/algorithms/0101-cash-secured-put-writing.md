## How it works

**Simple:** Keep cash in Treasury bills and every month sell someone the right to sell you the S&P 500 at today's price. You keep the fee; if the market falls, you have to buy at the higher price.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like selling home insurance: small regular premiums, and occasionally a large claim.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

Cboe's PUT index holds Treasury bills and sells a one-month at-the-money S&P 500 put each month, fully collateralised, held to expiry. The retail 'wheel' is a single-stock version that sells puts, takes the shares if assigned, then sells covered calls on them.

</details>

### The rule

On each monthly expiry, hold Treasury bills equal to the strike and sell one at-the-money one-month S&P 500 put; settle at expiry; repeat.

### Data needed

- Cboe PUT index daily history (free CSV)
- Treasury bill rates
- S&P 500 total return for comparison

### Why it might work

Index put buyers pay above the expected payout for crash insurance. Bondarenko reports that from 1990 to 2018 the VIX averaged 19.3% while realised S&P 500 volatility averaged 15.1% (as summarised in the Cboe post).

### Why it might fail

Losses come in crashes, when they hurt most. Single-stock wheels add stock-specific risk and assignment. The index ignores costs and taxes, and part of the history was built backwards before the index was launched.
