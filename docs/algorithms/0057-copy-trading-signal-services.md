## How it works

**Simple:** Instead of picking trades yourself, you let your account automatically copy another trader who has a good-looking record, or you pay for buy and sell alerts.

<details><summary>Analogy</summary>

Like copying the homework of the kid with the best grades last week, without knowing if they got lucky.

</details>

<details><summary>Precise</summary>

Platforms rank 'signal providers' by past return and let followers mirror their trades in proportion to account size. Rankings reward high recent returns, which favors high-risk strategies and survivors. Apesteguia, Oechssler and Weidenholzer (2020) show in an experiment that seeing others' success and being able to copy them increases risk taking.

</details>

### The rule

As commonly practiced: each quarter, allocate equally to the 5 providers with the highest trailing 12-month return and at least 12 months of history; copy all their trades proportionally; drop a provider if their drawdown exceeds 25 percent.

### Data needed

- Platform leaderboard histories (not freely available as a clean, survivor-free dataset)

### Why it might work

If skill persists, following past winners could work; Barber et al. (2004) find some persistence among a small group of Taiwanese day traders. Proponents claim the crowd surfaces skilled traders.

### Why it might fail

Apesteguia, Oechssler and Weidenholzer (2020) conclude copy trading 'leads to excessive risk taking'. Leaderboards suffer from survivorship and selection on luck. eToro's own required disclosure says about half of its retail CFD accounts lose money, which covers its copiers too.
