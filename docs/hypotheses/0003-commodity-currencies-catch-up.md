# Commodity currencies catch up to their commodity

## In plain words

Canada and Norway sell a lot of oil. When oil gets more expensive, their money usually gets stronger. The idea: if oil jumps and their money has not caught up yet, buy their money and wait for it to catch up. This is our most original idea and also the one most likely to fail.

## Claim

When the price of a country's main export moves and its currency has not yet followed, the currency tends to catch up over the following days to weeks. Starting pairs: the Canadian dollar with WTI crude, the Norwegian krone with Brent crude. Later: the Australian dollar with metals, the New Zealand dollar with dairy or a broad index.

This is our main attempt at something different from the usual retail strategies, and our way into commodities.

Code: `engine/src/parity/strategies/fx_commodity_gap.py` (strategy id `fx_commodity_gap`).

## Why it might be true

- Commodity prices explain much of the real exchange rate of commodity exporters (Chen and Rogoff, 2003).
- Country-specific commodity indices predicted 11 commodity currencies out of sample for up to two months (Kohlscheen, Avalos and Schrimpf, 2017).
- Different traders watch the oil market and the currency market, so information may take time to cross over.

## Why it might be false

- Oil and CAD move together on the same day, and the link fades at lower frequencies (Ferraro, Rogoff and Rossi, 2015). If the catch-up happens within hours, daily data will not see it.
- Currencies may lead commodities, not follow them (Chen, Rogoff and Rossi, 2010).
- After 2020 higher commodity prices came with a stronger US dollar, reversing the old pattern, probably because the US is now a net oil exporter (Rees, 2025). A rule fitted before 2020 may break after it.
- Oil supply shocks and demand shocks affect currencies differently (Kilian, 2009). A raw price mixes them.

## How we test it

1. Measure the plain lead-lag first: does yesterday's oil return predict today's or this week's CAD and NOK return?
2. Then the gap signal: residual of the currency's move after removing its estimated oil sensitivity, summed over 20 days.
3. Report the rank IC at 1, 5, 21 and 63 days to see at what horizon any information sits.
4. Split before and after 2020.
5. Backtest only once we know the horizon, so we do not tune the holding period on returns.

## Result so far

[EXP-0001](../experiments/0001-baseline-backtests.md): the first version fails as a strategy (Sharpe 0.06, and it loses money at 15% volatility). But its information coefficient rises with horizon, from 0.02 at one day to 0.125 at 63 days, while the strategy rebalances weekly and turns over its book 12 times a year. The signal may be real at a slower horizon than the one we trade. The 63-day figure rests on about 25 yearly values with overlapping returns, so we treat it as a lead, not a finding. This is the next thing to look at.

[EXP-0002](../experiments/0002-baseline-gauntlet.md), the gauntlet: fails all six tests against the S&P 500 and four of five reality tests. As built, there is no evidence of a real effect. Every monthly variant beat its weekly twin, which matches the IC pattern, so a slower version is now its own hypothesis, [HYP-0008](0008-monthly-commodity-gap.md), with this sweep counted as trials.
