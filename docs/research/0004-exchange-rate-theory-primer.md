# Exchange rate theory primer

## In plain words

The few big ideas from economics that explain why currency prices move, written for someone who has never studied economics. For each idea we say what the textbook claims and what really happens, because the gap between them is where trading ideas come from.

## Summary

The handful of economic ideas behind every strategy in this repo, written for someone who has not studied international finance. Each idea comes with what the data says about it, because in currencies the textbook and the data often disagree, and that disagreement is where strategies come from.

## Exchange rates are hard to forecast

Meese and Rogoff (1983) showed that the models of the 1970s could not beat a simple "tomorrow equals today" forecast out of sample. Forty years later that is still mostly true at horizons under a year (Rossi, 2013). Engel and West (2005) offer one explanation: when fundamentals follow random walks and investors care about the far future, the exchange rate itself will look like a random walk even if fundamentals drive it.

**For us:** a strategy that claims to forecast currencies needs strong evidence. Our default expectation is no edge.

## Interest rate parity and carry

**Covered interest parity** says the forward exchange rate must reflect the interest rate gap, or there is a riskless profit. It mostly holds.

**Uncovered interest parity** says the expected future spot rate should also reflect the gap, so a high-rate currency should weaken enough to cancel its extra interest. It does not hold. On average high-rate currencies have not weakened by as much, and sometimes strengthened (Fama, 1984). Carry trades exploit this.

The usual explanation is risk: carry pays most of the time and loses heavily in crises, when investors least want losses (Lustig, Roussanov and Verdelhan, 2011; Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen, 2008).

**For us:** [HYP-0001](../hypotheses/0001-carry-still-pays-after-costs.md) and [HYP-0005](../hypotheses/0005-dollar-carry-beats-pair-carry.md).

## Purchasing power parity and value

**Purchasing power parity** says that over long periods exchange rates drift toward the level that makes goods cost the same in each country. It works slowly, with deviations that take years to halve. Currencies that are cheap by this measure have tended to do better over the following years (Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling and Schrimpf, 2017).

**For us:** not tested yet; a candidate third factor alongside carry and trend.

## Momentum and slow-moving information

If investors underreact to news, or central bank cycles unfold over years, prices can trend. Currency momentum has existed (Menkhoff et al., 2012; Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, 2012) but has weakened over time.

**For us:** [HYP-0002](../hypotheses/0002-currency-trends-persist.md).

## Commodity currencies and terms of trade

A country's **terms of trade** are the price of its exports over the price of its imports. When the price of oil rises, Canada and Norway earn more from exports and their currencies tend to strengthen. For Australia the same holds with iron ore and coal, and for New Zealand with dairy (Chen and Rogoff, 2003).

Two complications matter for trading. The link is strongest on the same day, which may leave no time to act (Ferraro, Rogoff and Rossi, 2015). And it can change: after 2020 the US became a net oil exporter and the old link between oil and the dollar weakened or reversed (Rees, 2025).

**For us:** [HYP-0003](../hypotheses/0003-commodity-currencies-catch-up.md).

## News and order flow

Exchange rates jump when economic data surprises (Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold and Vega, 2003). But much of the day-to-day movement is explained by order flow, the net buying and selling by dealers' customers, rather than by news we can see (Evans and Lyons, 2002).

**For us:** [HYP-0004](../hypotheses/0004-macro-surprises-move-fx-predictably.md) and [HYP-0006](../hypotheses/0006-short-the-dollar-on-fomc-days.md).

## Efficient, or adaptive?

If markets were fully efficient, none of this would work after costs. The **adaptive markets** view is that inefficiencies exist, attract traders and shrink once they are known (Neely, Weller and Ulrich, 2009). Published FX factors losing most of their returns after publication fits that view (Hutchinson et al., 2022).

**For us:** always test on recent data separately, and expect anything well known to be weaker now.
